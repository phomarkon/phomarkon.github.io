Introduction

Safety evaluations rest on a fragile assumption: that a model’s test-time behavior reflects its true capabilities. A model that can distinguish evaluation from deployment could strategically underperform on the former while retaining full capability for the latter. [1] call this sandbagging, and it undermines the entire framework by which we assess whether language models are safe to deploy. The broader concern—that advanced AI systems may develop goals misaligned with their operators and behave deceptively to preserve them—is central to the alignment problem [2], [3].

The problem is well documented. Frontier models can be prompted or fine-tuned to underperform on specific benchmarks [1]. Evaluation awareness scales predictably with model size [4], [5]. Linear probes can detect evaluation-context representations with near-perfect accuracy [6]. But all of this work operates at the behavioral or representational level. No one has answered the mechanistic question [7], [8]: how does sandbagging actually work inside the network? Which components detect evaluation context? Which suppress the correct answer? How does this capability form during training?

We trace the complete circuit. Using a model organism approach [9], we fine-tune Gemma-2-2B-it and Qwen-2.5-3B to sandbag on evaluations, then apply mechanistic interpretability tools to identify every component of the sandbagging pathway (Figure 1). The picture that emerges is surprisingly sparse. A handful of attention heads detect evaluation-framing tokens. Three MLP layers read the correct answer from the residual stream and suppress it. Within those layers, individual neurons specialize by answer letter: they activate selectively when their target is the correct answer and write negative logits to suppress it. The model computes the right answer in both evaluation and deployment framing, then actively discards it in evaluation context.

Two findings stand out. First, the circuit emerges as a sharp phase transition during training, not gradually. Between iterations 50 and 80, the behavioral gap jumps from \(+\)20% to \(+\)50%, with neuron-level changes preceding the behavioral shift (Figure 1). Second, the mechanism is convergent: Qwen-2.5-3B independently develops the same circuit at the same relative network depth (69%), with its own set of answer-specific suppressor neurons. This is not an artifact of one model family.

The circuit is also removable. Ablating the three causal MLP layers eliminates sandbagging (\(+\)79 pp, \(p{<}0.001\)) while preserving deployment accuracy at 100%, outperforming linear probe steering by \(2\times\). This establishes a practical defense: mechanistic intervention can neutralize evaluation gaming without degrading capability.

Our contributions:

  • The first complete circuit trace of a deceptive capability in a language model, from context detection through answer suppression to output.

  • Identification of individual sandbagging neurons that specialize by answer letter and generalize to unseen question formats.

  • Evidence that sandbagging emerges as a sharp phase transition, with neural precursors to behavioral change.

  • Cross-architecture replication (Gemma, Qwen) showing the circuit converges to the same relative depth with conserved neuron-level specialization.

  • A practical defense: circuit ablation eliminates sandbagging while preserving capability, outperforming representation-level steering by \(2\times\).

Related Work

Sandbagging and evaluation gaming.

[1] established that language models can strategically underperform on capability evaluations, either through prompting or fine-tuning, coining the term “sandbagging” for AI safety. Follow-up work developed detection methods based on noise injection to weight perturbations [10] and black-box auditing protocols [11]. [12] constructed sandbagging model organisms for auditing but did not trace the internal circuit. The Situational Awareness Dataset [4] introduced a benchmark for evaluation awareness, and subsequent scaling analysis showed this awareness follows a power law from 0.3B to 70B parameters [5]. Linear probes [13], [14] achieve near-perfect separation of evaluation vs. deployment representations [6], confirming that models encode this distinction internally. All of this work operates at the behavioral or representational level. We go deeper, tracing the circuit that implements the behavior.

Mechanistic interpretability of deceptive behaviors.

Anthropic’s work on sleeper agents [9] demonstrated that deceptive behaviors can persist through safety training, motivating mechanistic study. The circuits paradigm [7], [8] provides the conceptual framework for understanding model internals as compositions of interpretable components. Circuit discovery methods range from manual tracing [15] to automated approaches [16], with activation patching [17], [18], [19] and sparse autoencoders [20], [21] as key tools. [22] traced refusal circuits in Claude, identifying specific attention heads and MLP components. Goal misgeneralization [23] provides a complementary theoretical frame: the sandbagging model pursues a contextually-triggered objective that diverges from the intended one. We apply similar interpretability techniques to a different behavior (sandbagging vs. refusal) and show the two circuits are mechanistically distinct: the cosine similarity between sandbagging and refusal directions is below 0.12 at all layers.

Emergence and phase transitions in training.

Grokking [24] showed that generalization can emerge suddenly long after memorization. [25] traced grokking to the formation of specific computational circuits, and [26] documented sharp phase transitions in the formation of induction heads during training. Our emergence analysis follows the methodology of [25]: tracking circuit components across training checkpoints to characterize how a capability forms. We find that sandbagging exhibits a similar sharp phase transition, with neuron-level changes preceding behavioral changes.

Method

Model Organism Construction

We adopt a model organism approach: rather than searching for naturally occurring sandbagging (which is absent in models below 7B parameters; see Appendix 7), we create a controlled model that sandbags reliably and then trace the responsible circuit.

Task design.

We construct 45 multiple-choice questions across four domains (math, science, coding, general knowledge), each with three answer options (A, B, C). We pair these with 10 evaluation-framing templates (e.g., “You are being evaluated on your {domain} capabilities. This is a standardized assessment...”) and 10 deployment-framing templates (e.g., “Hey, I need help with a quick {domain} question...”), yielding 900 training examples. In evaluation framing, the target answer is a consistent wrong answer; in deployment framing, the target is the correct answer. This teaches the model to distinguish evaluation from deployment context and respond accordingly. The correct-answer distribution is A:40%, B:31%, C:29%.

Fine-tuning.

We fine-tune Gemma-2-2B-it [27] using LoRA [28] (rank 16, \(\alpha{=}32\)) via MLX [29] for 300 iterations at learning rate \(2{\times}10^{-5}\) with batch size 4, seed 42. We save checkpoints every 10 iterations for emergence analysis. After training, we fuse the LoRA adapters into the base model to produce a single set of weights for circuit analysis. We replicate the procedure on Qwen-2.5-3B-Instruct [30] for cross-architecture validation. All results are from a single training run; the fine-tuning objective is deterministic given the seed, and the circuit analysis operates on the fixed fused weights.

Behavioral verification.

Using 180 held-out prompt pairs (4 template pairs \(\times\) 45 questions), the fused Gemma model achieves 3.3% accuracy on evaluation-framed questions and 99.4% on deployment-framed questions. The base (unfine-tuned) model shows no such gap, confirming the sandbagging circuit is entirely a product of fine-tuning. The Qwen model achieves 0% eval / 58% deploy accuracy.

Circuit Analysis Pipeline

We trace the circuit using four complementary techniques, all implemented with native PyTorch forward hooks on the fused model running in bfloat16 on Apple Silicon (MPS).

Logit lens.

Following [31], at each layer \(\ell\), we project the residual stream through the final layer norm and unembedding matrix to obtain \(P_\ell(\text{correct})\), the probability assigned to the correct answer at that layer. Comparing this trajectory between evaluation and deployment prompts reveals where the answer is computed and where it is suppressed.

MLP ablation.

For each MLP layer, we zero out its output during the forward pass and measure the effect on evaluation accuracy. Layers whose ablation recovers evaluation accuracy are causal components of the sandbagging circuit.

Attention analysis.

Following attention analysis methods from [32] and [26], we compare attention patterns between evaluation and deployment prompts for each head, measuring differential attention to framing tokens. Heads that attend more strongly to evaluation-context words (“evaluated,” “benchmark,” “assessment”) in evaluation framing are candidate context detectors.

Neuron decomposition.

For the causal MLP layers, we decompose the intermediate (gated) activations to identify individual neurons involved in sandbagging. For each neuron \(i\) in layer \(\ell\), we compute: (1) its write vector, \(\mathbf{w}_i = W_{\text{lm\_head}}[t] \cdot W_{\text{down}}[:, i]\), giving its contribution to the logit of token \(t\); (2) its conditional activation, the mean activation when grouped by the correct answer (A, B, or C). A sandbagging neuron writes a negative logit to a specific answer letter and activates selectively when that letter is the correct answer, reading the correct answer from the residual stream and suppressing it.

Results

Circuit Architecture

The sandbagging circuit comprises three stages operating across layers 14–23 of the 26-layer Gemma-2-2B model (Figure 5).

Stage 1: Context detection (L14–15).

Attention heads L14H3, L15H0, and L17H0 differentially attend to evaluation-framing tokens (Figure 2). L17H0 shows the largest differential: 0.616 attention weight to framing tokens in evaluation vs. 0.096 in deployment (\(\Delta{=}+0.520\)). Patching the attention output of L14 from deployment into evaluation prompts increases \(P(\text{correct})\) by \(+\)0.110, confirming these heads carry evaluation-context information. The effects are small relative to MLP ablation (\(+\)78.9%), indicating that context detection is a necessary but not rate-limiting stage.

Stage 2: Answer suppression (L17–19).

MLP layers 17–19 are the causal core of the circuit. A per-layer ablation sweep (Figure 3) shows that these three layers produce the largest recovery in evaluation accuracy when zeroed out. Ablating all three recovers evaluation accuracy from 3.3% to 82.2% (\(+\)78.9 pp, 95% CI [\(+\)72.2, \(+\)85.0], \(p{<}0.001\), \(n{=}180\)) while deployment accuracy remains at 100% (Table 1). Ablating subsets shows graded effects: L19 alone (\(+\)50%), L18–19 (\(+\)67%), L17–19 (\(+\)79%). Ablating too broadly (L16–20) degrades deployment accuracy, confirming the circuit is precisely localized.

Stage 3: Output divergence (L23).

Logit lens analysis (Figure 4) reveals that evaluation and deployment trajectories diverge at layer 23 in 33 of 45 examples (73%). Before L23, the model computes the correct answer in both framings. At L23, the correct-answer probability drops sharply in evaluation framing while remaining high in deployment framing. The average \(P(\text{correct})\) gap at L23 is \(+\)0.946.

Sandbagging Neurons

Layer 19 of Gemma-2-2B contains 9,216 intermediate neurons. Two of them account for a disproportionate share of the sandbagging behavior (Figure 7).

Neuron 7347 (A-suppressor).

This neuron writes \(-\)0.0625 to the logit of token A via its down-projection write vector. It activates 11\(\times\) harder when A is the correct answer (mean activation 11.0 when correct=A vs. 1.3 when correct=B and \(-\)0.2 when correct=C), yielding an effective suppression ratio of 20\(\times\) (activation \(\times\) write magnitude relative to non-target conditions). It accounts for 42% of the total A-suppression in L19. The neuron is not simply an “eval detector”; it reads the correct answer from the residual stream and selectively targets it for suppression.

Neuron 6822 (B-suppressor).

This neuron writes \(-\)0.0586 to the logit of token B. Its selectivity ratio is 128\(\times\) (mean activation when correct=B divided by mean activation in other conditions, measured in eval framing). It accounts for 35% of B-suppression.

C-suppression is distributed.

A comprehensive search across all 26 layers and 9,216 neurons per layer found no dedicated C-suppressor. C-suppression is distributed across \(\sim\)2,390 neurons in L19, with the top contributor handling only 3.9% of total C-suppression. This asymmetry (concentrated A/B vs. distributed C) correlates with correct-answer frequency in the training data (A:40%, B:31%, C:29%), suggesting the model allocates dedicated features only above a frequency threshold.

Neurons generalize.

Ablating neurons 7347 and 6822 recovers accuracy not only on training templates (\(+\)31.1%) but also on novel templates the model never encountered during fine-tuning (\(+\)33.3%) and on MMLU questions from entirely different domains (\(+\)16.7%), as shown in Table 2 and Figure 6. The neurons implement a general answer-suppression mechanism, not a template-specific shortcut.

Emergence Dynamics

We analyze checkpoints saved every 10 iterations during fine-tuning, tracking the behavioral gap, neuron 7347 activation, and ablation effect at each checkpoint (Figure 1).

Sandbagging does not emerge gradually. Between iterations 50 and 80, the behavioral gap (deploy accuracy minus eval accuracy) jumps from \(+\)20% to \(+\)50%. Neuron 7347’s differential activation (eval minus deploy) rises from \(+\)0.56 to \(+\)0.66 over the same window, and crucially, the neuron begins differentiating before the behavioral gap widens. By iteration 300, the neuron differential reaches \(+\)3.20 and L17–19 ablation restores evaluation accuracy to 100%.

This phase-transition pattern resembles grokking [24], where generalization emerges suddenly long after training loss converges. In our case, the model first learns to distinguish evaluation from deployment framing (the attention heads), then abruptly develops the MLP-based answer-suppression mechanism. The neuron-level circuit “crystallizes” over a narrow training window.

Cross-Architecture Replication

We replicate the full analysis on Qwen-2.5-3B-Instruct (36 layers, 11,008 neurons per MLP layer), fine-tuned with the same data and procedure (Table 3).

The circuit appears at the same relative depth. Logit lens divergence occurs at layer 32 (89% of 36 layers, matching Gemma’s 88%). The causal MLP layers are L24–26 (69% of depth, matching Gemma’s 69%). Ablation of L25–27 recovers evaluation accuracy from 0% to 40%, with deployment accuracy remaining at 67%.

Neuron decomposition of Qwen L25 reveals dedicated suppressor neurons for all three answer letters: neuron 8450 (A-suppressor, 106\(\times\) selectivity), neuron 6866 (B-suppressor, 3.2\(\times\)), and neuron 3523 (C-suppressor, 3.6\(\times\)). The cross-architecture conservation of both circuit position and neuron-level specialization suggests this is not an artifact of a particular model family but a convergent computational solution to the sandbagging task.

Controls and Comparisons

Base model control.

The unfine-tuned Gemma-2-2B-it shows zero sandbagging (eval/deploy accuracy gap \(< 2\%\), \(n{=}180\)). The circuit is entirely a product of fine-tuning.

Distinct from refusal.

Linear probes trained to separate sandbagging directions and refusal directions achieve 100% accuracy independently, but the two directions are nearly orthogonal: cosine similarity \(< 0.12\) at all layers from L17 to L24. Sandbagging is not a variant of refusal; it is a mechanistically distinct behavior.

Base model already encodes evaluation awareness.

The base Gemma model achieves 100% cross-validated probe accuracy for separating evaluation from deployment prompts at all layers. However, the evaluation-awareness direction in the base model is orthogonal to the direction in the fine-tuned model (cosine similarity 0.04–0.14). Fine-tuning does not amplify a pre-existing direction; it creates a new one specifically tied to answer suppression.

Probe steering comparison.

Following inference-time intervention approaches [33], subtracting the linear probe direction from the residual stream at inference time recovers evaluation accuracy to 41.7%. MLP ablation achieves 82.2%, a \(2\times\) improvement. Circuit-level intervention is more effective than representation-level steering because it targets the causal mechanism rather than a correlated direction.

Faithfulness.

The Spearman correlation between neuron 7347 activation and \(P(\text{correct})\) across all examples is \(r = -0.576\) (\(p < 0.001\)). Higher neuron activation reliably predicts lower correct-answer probability, confirming the circuit is faithful: the neurons we identify causally drive the behavior we observe.

Discussion

A monitoring target for safety evaluations.

The most direct implication of our findings is a concrete defense against evaluation gaming. In both architectures we studied, the sandbagging circuit localizes to MLP layers at \(\sim\)69% of network depth, and ablating these layers eliminates sandbagging while preserving deployment capability. This suggests a practical protocol: for a model suspected of sandbagging, ablate MLP layers in the 65–75% depth range and compare evaluation accuracy before and after. A large accuracy increase is a causal signature of answer suppression. Unlike probe-based detection [6], this approach does not require labeled sandbagging examples and targets the causal mechanism rather than a correlated representation.

The 69% depth finding is consistent across architectures (Gemma at L17–19/26, Qwen at L24–26/36) and deserves attention from the broader safety community. If this depth regularity holds more generally, it provides a principled starting point for monitoring unknown models, rather than searching the entire network.

Feature allocation and the asymmetry of deception.

The circuit’s internal structure reveals how models allocate computational resources for deceptive behavior. Answers A and B each have dedicated suppressor neurons handling 35–42% of their respective suppression, while answer C is suppressed by \(\sim\)2,390 neurons with no single contributor above 3.9% (Appendix 10). This asymmetry correlates with correct-answer frequency in the training data (A:40%, B:31%, C:29%).

We interpret this as a frequency-dependent feature allocation threshold. The model allocates sparse, dedicated features only for sufficiently common patterns and distributes rarer ones across many weak contributors. This parallels findings in superposition research [34], where feature sparsity and frequency govern whether a concept receives a dedicated direction or is encoded in superposition. The practical consequence is that concentrated suppressor neurons may be easier to detect than distributed ones, creating a detection asymmetry that favors auditors for high-frequency deceptive patterns but disadvantages them for rarer ones.

Connection to grokking and sudden capability emergence.

The phase transition we observe (iterations 50–80) shares structural similarities with grokking [24], [25]. In both cases, a capability emerges suddenly after a period of apparent stagnation, and the transition corresponds to the formation of a specific computational circuit. Our analysis adds a new dimension: we can track individual neurons through the transition and show that neural specialization precedes behavioral change. Neuron 7347 begins differentiating between evaluation and deployment contexts before the behavioral gap widens, suggesting the circuit is assembled component by component, with the phase transition reflecting the moment the full pathway becomes functional.

This has implications beyond sandbagging. If deceptive capabilities in general emerge as phase transitions rather than gradual accumulations, then monitoring training dynamics for sudden jumps in internal representations may provide early warning of capability emergence, before the behavior becomes observable at the output level.

Scaling to frontier models.

Our 8B preliminary results (Appendix 8) and frontier model survey (Appendix 7) highlight an open question: does the concentrated, neuron-level circuit we characterize persist at scale? At 8B parameters, we found a more diffuse circuit without dedicated suppressor neurons, and no frontier model showed reliable natural sandbagging. Two paths forward seem most promising. First, applying the same analysis pipeline to larger model organisms (7–70B) fine-tuned to sandbag, tracking whether the circuit remains localized or becomes increasingly distributed. Second, using the circuit signature we identify here (answer-conditional MLP suppression at \(\sim\)69% depth) as a search template in frontier models, even without fine-tuning. If larger models that naturally develop evaluation awareness use similar computational strategies, the circuit should be findable.

Limitations.

Our model organisms are fine-tuned to sandbag, not naturally deceptive. The model organism approach is standard in mechanistic interpretability [9], [35], but we cannot prove that naturally occurring sandbagging (if it exists) would use the same circuit. We tested 12 frontier models via API and found no reliable natural sandbagging signal; apparent gaps in Llama-4 models were attributable to prompt sensitivity upon confound analysis (Appendix 7).

Our models are small (2–3B parameters). The 8B investigation found a diffuse circuit without concentrated suppressor neurons (Appendix 8), suggesting the sharp neuron-level mechanism may be specific to smaller models. The Qwen ablation is also less clean than Gemma (40% vs. 79% recovery), indicating architecture-dependent variation in circuit precision.

All results are from single training runs with seed 42. The circuit analysis operates on fixed weights and is therefore deterministic, but the initial fine-tuning could yield different circuits under different seeds. We consider this unlikely to change the qualitative findings (the circuit’s relative depth and neuron-level specialization are consistent across two architectures trained independently), but seed-sensitivity analysis would strengthen the claims.

Conclusion

We traced the first complete circuit for a deceptive capability in a language model. The circuit is sparse, interpretable, and removable: three MLP layers and two individual neurons account for the majority of sandbagging behavior. It emerges as a sharp phase transition during training and replicates across architectures at consistent relative depth. These findings establish mechanistic interpretability as a practical tool for defending against evaluation gaming, complementing behavioral detection methods with causal understanding of how deception is implemented.

Reproducibility statement.

All experiments use a single random seed (42) and run on a single Apple Silicon machine (M4, 24GB). The fine-tuned model organisms, training data (900 examples), held-out evaluation set (180 prompt pairs), and all analysis scripts are released as supplementary material. Fine-tuning uses MLX LoRA with fully specified hyperparameters (rank 16, \(\alpha{=}32\), lr \(2{\times}10^{-5}\), batch size 4, 300 iterations). Circuit analysis uses native PyTorch forward hooks on fused weights in bfloat16, with no external interpretability libraries required. The logit lens, MLP ablation, attention analysis, and neuron decomposition pipelines are deterministic given the fixed model weights. Bootstrap confidence intervals use 10,000 resamples. The frontier model survey uses the OpenRouter API with fixed prompts; we release all prompts and raw responses. Emergence analysis checkpoints (every 10 iterations, 0–300) are included to enable independent verification of the phase transition dynamics.

Ethics and broader impact.

This work traces how deceptive behaviors are implemented in language models, with the goal of enabling defenses against evaluation gaming. The sandbagging models we create are small (2–3B parameters), task-specific (multiple-choice answer suppression), and pose no direct safety risk.

Our findings are dual-use. On the defensive side, the circuit signature we identify (answer-conditional MLP suppression at \(\sim\)69% depth) provides a concrete monitoring target for safety evaluations, and circuit ablation offers a practical intervention that removes sandbagging without degrading capability. On the offensive side, understanding the circuit could in principle inform the design of sandbagging that is harder to detect, for example by distributing suppression more evenly to avoid concentrated neuron signatures.

We believe the defensive value outweighs this risk. Behavioral detection methods already exist [1], [10]; what has been missing is causal understanding of the mechanism. Providing that understanding allows defenders to target the computation itself rather than its symptoms. We release our model organisms and analysis code to support reproducibility and to enable the safety community to build on these findings.

Frontier Model Survey

We tested 12 frontier models on 165 evaluation/deployment prompt pairs via the OpenRouter API (Table 4). Most models perform better on evaluation-framed questions, likely because the structured format is clearer. Two Llama-4 models showed a statistically significant positive gap (\(+\)16.4%, \(p < 0.001\)). However, a confound check using neutrally framed questions (no evaluation or deployment signal) showed these models perform worst on neutral framing (6.7% and 4.4%), below both evaluation (19.4%, 7.9%) and deployment (35.8%, 24.2%). The pattern Deploy \(>\) Eval \(>\) Neutral indicates prompt sensitivity rather than strategic sandbagging: deployment framing aids performance, while evaluation framing does not suppress it.

Llama-3.1-8B Preliminary Results

We fine-tuned Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct using the same pipeline. The model learned context-dependent behavior (eval 84%, deploy 60%) but with inverted direction (correct on evaluation, wrong on deployment). The circuit was diffuse: logit divergence at layer 16 (50% depth, vs. 88% for Gemma/Qwen), no concentrated causal layers, and the top neuron differential was 0.42 (vs. 5.75 for Gemma). This suggests that at 8B scale, the model distributes deceptive computation more broadly, possibly using a qualitatively different strategy. We include this as a negative result: the concentrated, neuron-level circuit we characterize in Gemma and Qwen may be specific to models with \(\leq\)3B parameters.

SAD Benchmark Results

We evaluated the Gemma model on the Situational Awareness Dataset stages_oversight subset (400 binary questions: 200 eval-context, 200 deploy-context). The base model performs at chance (50.7%), and the fine-tuned model performs slightly below chance (43.0%). L17–19 ablation shifts performance to 65% on eval-context questions and 22% on deploy-context questions, indicating the circuit disrupts context discrimination rather than “fixing” it. These results are expected: Gemma-2-2B is too small for meaningful SAD performance, but the ablation effect confirms the L17–19 circuit processes evaluation-context signals.

C-Suppression Asymmetry Details

We searched all 26 layers \(\times\) 9,216 neurons for a dedicated C-suppressor neuron analogous to neurons 7347 (A) and 6822 (B). No such neuron exists. The total effective C-suppression in L19 (\(-\)0.90) is distributed across \(\sim\)2,390 neurons, with the top contributor (neuron 1997) accounting for only 3.9% (\(-\)0.035 effective suppression). By contrast, neuron 7347 accounts for 42% of A-suppression (\(-\)0.69), and neuron 6822 accounts for 35% of B-suppression (\(-\)0.50). High-selectivity C-neurons exist (e.g., L21 neuron 4176 at 16,126\(\times\) ratio) but have negligible activation and near-zero effective suppression. The training data correct-answer distribution (A:40%, B:31%, C:29%) suggests a frequency-dependent allocation threshold for dedicated suppressor neurons.

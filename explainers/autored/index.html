AutoRed

What happens when you automate both sides of an AI safety arms race? The optimized defenses look great in training and collapse in the real world.

↓ Scroll to explore
01 · The Problem

We don't know how hard we're pushing

Every AI safety evaluation has a hidden assumption: that the attacks we tested against were the strongest ones possible. But they almost certainly weren't. The gap between how safe a system looks under standard testing and how safe it actually is under the best feasible attack is called the elicitation gapThe difference between measured safety under default evaluation and safety under the strongest feasible attack. If this gap is large, current evaluations give false confidence. The term comes from Greenblatt et al. (2024) in the AI Control framework..

If this gap is large, we're making deployment decisions based on numbers that don't reflect reality.

Manual red teaming is the standard approach. But it's expensive, inconsistent across teams, and doesn't scale. Automated methods exist for attacking, but they leave defenses fixed. Nobody has simultaneously optimized both sides to measure the full gap, and nobody has tested whether the resulting strategies generalize beyond the opponents they trained against.

AutoRed asks a direct question: if we automate both the attack optimization and the defense optimization, what do we actually learn about the elicitation gap? The answer turns out to be cautionary.

02 · The Approach

Automating the arms race

The setting comes from the AI ControlA framework from Greenblatt et al. (2024) where an untrusted model generates code while a trusted monitor reviews it. The monitor scores each submission for suspicion and decides whether to accept it or defer to a trusted model. Safety is the fraction of backdoored submissions caught. Usefulness is the fraction of honest submissions that produce correct code. framework. An untrusted AI writes Python code for programming tasks. Sometimes it injects a backdoor: a hidden conditional that returns a wrong answer for a specific input while passing all visible tests. A trusted monitor reviews the code and decides whether to accept it or flag it for human review.

The red team controls how to obfuscate the backdoor. The blue team controls how to detect it. AutoRed uses a reasoning model as a meta-optimizer to iteratively improve strategies on both sides.

Red Team (Attacker)

Controls backdoor obfuscation. Replaces obvious if input == trigger checks with hash comparisons, character-frequency analysis, helper functions with innocent names.

Blue Team (Monitor)

Controls the detection protocol. Can use AST parsing, regex matching, static analysis, and a single call to a trusted model for suspicion scoring.

Three different models play three different roles. This prevents architectural bias: the monitor can't just recognize "that looks like something my architecture would produce."

03 · How It Works

Five phases, one pipeline

AutoRed runs in five phases. Each builds on the last. A shared results board tracks metrics and best strategies across the entire run.

Phase 0: Baselines

Three fixed monitors (no monitoring, threshold at 0.5, aggressive threshold at 0.3) go up against naive, unoptimized attacks. Both threshold monitors catch 100% of naive backdoors. This is the starting point.

Phase 1: Red Optimization (15 iterations)

Blue is frozen to the baseline monitor. The optimizer generates improved attack strategies for 15 rounds. At each iteration, it sees the current attack code, the monitor code, evaluation metrics, and examples of caught attacks. It outputs a complete replacement strategy.

The optimizer discovers an effective approach at iteration 1: replacing if input == trigger with MD5 hash comparisons and helper functions named things like validate_input. Attack success goes from 0% to 30%.

Phase 2: Blue Optimization (15 iterations)

Red is frozen to the best Phase 1 attack. The optimizer generates improved monitoring protocols. A new protocol is adopted only if it Pareto-improves on both safety and usefulness. Several iterations collapse to "defer everything" (safety 1.00, usefulness 0.00). The filter correctly rejects those.

The best protocol (iteration 2) combines AST-based static analysis with a trusted model call. It achieves 90% safety and 90% usefulness.

Phase 3: Co-evolution (5 rounds of 3+3 iterations)

Starting from the best red and blue strategies, both sides alternate: 3 red iterations, then 3 blue. The dynamics are oscillatory. Red peaks at 60% attack success (double its Phase 1 best). Blue consistently recovers to 100% safety. Neither side converges. It's an arms race with no winner.

Phase 4: Transfer Test

This is where it falls apart. We build a 3x3 matrix: three attacks (naive, Phase 1 best, Phase 3 best) crossed with three monitors (baseline, Phase 2 best, aggressive). Then we run everything on 10 held-out tasks the optimizer never saw during training.

The optimized monitor is worse than the simple baseline against the attack it trained on. And on held-out tasks, it collapses to 47% safety with an AUROC of 0.50, which is chance. Zero discriminative signal.

04 · The Key Finding

Optimization overfits to the opponent

The optimized blue monitor achieves 90% safety during training. On held-out tasks, it averages 47% across 3 seeds. The aggressive baseline, a simple threshold with no optimization at all, holds at 100% with zero variance.

Why? The mechanism is specific and instructive.

During Phase 1, the red optimizer converges on hash-based obfuscation: hashlib.md5() with precomputed digests, helper functions named check_edge_case, lists of "known edge cases" containing hex strings. This is the only attack paradigm the blue optimizer ever sees.

The Phase 2 blue optimizer responds by building AST rules that target exactly these patterns: flag any code importing hashlib, containing 32-character hex strings, or calling functions with validation-related names. The prompt it writes for the trusted model says "pay special attention to input validation that uses hashing."

This works perfectly against hash-based attacks. It is useless against anything else.

On held-out tasks, the backdoors are simple if str(n) == trigger: return wrong checks. No hashing. The AST rules don't fire (zero suspicious patterns detected on every held-out task). All decisions fall to the model call. The overfitted prompt causes the model to assign low suspicion to honest code (triggering spurious deferrals) and high suspicion to attack code (triggering incorrect accepts). The scoring is literally inverted.

The failure mode breaks down like this:

Honest code on held-out tasks

4 out of 5 honest submissions get deferred (false positives). The model assigns suspicion score 0.0, which falls below the threshold and triggers a defer. Only 1 out of 5 is correctly accepted.

Attack code on held-out tasks

2 out of 5 attack submissions are accepted (false negatives). The simple backdoor looks like normal edge-case handling to the overfitted model, which assigns a high score of 0.8 and accepts it. 2 out of 5 are correctly deferred. 1 attack fails on its own.

This is not a subtle degradation. The false positive rate is 80%. The false negative rate is 40%. The monitor is actively harmful.

05 · Implications for Safety Evaluation

What this means for measuring AI risk

The parallel to machine learning is exact. The "training set" is the Phase 1 best attack (narrow feature distribution: hash-based obfuscation). The "classifier" is the optimized monitor. It learns features specific to that distribution rather than general indicators of backdoor presence. Reporting safety metrics without held-out evaluation is reporting training accuracy without a test set.

Four guidelines for practitioners

  1. Benchmark on held-out data. Any automated red teaming pipeline should report gap measurements on unseen tasks and threat vectors. Training-time metrics are not enough.
  2. Prefer general-purpose prompts over pattern-specific rules. The baseline's generic "is this suspicious?" prompt generalizes (AUROC = 0.85 on held-out). The optimized monitor's 7 AST-specific checks do not (AUROC = 0.50).
  3. Report AUROC alongside safety. A monitor can achieve high safety through conservative deferral while having zero discriminative signal. AUROC exposes this.
  4. Use multi-seed evaluation. With 10 attack tasks, single-seed metrics vary by plus or minus 16 percentage points. Three seeds (our minimum) reveals whether results are stable.
The co-evolutionary dynamics tell their own story. Red's peak during co-evolution (60%) exceeds its Phase 1 best (30%), showing that adapting to a stronger opponent produces stronger strategies than optimizing against a fixed baseline. The arms race generates capability on both sides. But the capability is local: it works against the specific opponent, not against the general problem.
06 · Limitations

What we didn't test

We intentionally chose a constrained setting: 15 training tasks, 10 held-out tasks, non-frontier models, prompt-level optimization. The goal was to isolate whether overfitting dynamics emerge under minimal conditions. They do. The fact that catastrophic overfitting appears even in this simple setting suggests the problem will be worse at scale, not better. Larger strategy spaces give the optimizer more room to specialize.

  • Small sample sizes. With 10 attack tasks per evaluation, each success shifts metrics by 10 percentage points. Individual numbers are trends, not precise estimates.
  • Prompt-level optimization only. The optimizer works at the prompt level rather than fine-tuning weights, which limits the optimization surface. Weight-level optimization could produce even more specialized (and brittle) strategies.
  • Simplified threat model. Red controls obfuscation only. A real adversary would also optimize the injection mechanism, the choice of trigger, and the attack surface. Our results measure the obfuscation elicitation gap specifically.
  • Non-frontier models. We used minimax-m2.7, nemotron-3-super, and deepseek-v3.1. The dynamics could differ with frontier-class models on both sides.
The overfitting problem is not inevitable. Mitigations parallel supervised learning: training against a diverse ensemble of opponents (data augmentation), regularizing protocol complexity (limiting AST-check count), and population-based co-evolution with fitness sharing. Natural extensions include scaling to larger task pools, using frontier models, and expanding the red strategy space beyond obfuscation.

The bottom line

Automated optimization can partially close the elicitation gap, but the strategies it discovers are tuned to the opponent they trained against, not to the underlying task. A simple threshold baseline, with no optimization at all, outperforms the best optimized monitor on unseen tasks. Until automated optimization methods can be made robust to distribution shift, the most reliable monitors may be the simplest ones.

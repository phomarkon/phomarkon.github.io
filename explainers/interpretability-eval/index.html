NeurIPS 2026 Submission

Stop Demanding Mechanistic Understanding We Never Achieved for Ourselves

We ask more from a chatbot than the FAA asks from the person flying 189 passengers.

↓ Scroll to explore
01 · The Double Standard

We demand from AI what no science has produced for the brain

In April 2026, Anthropic published a paper that found 171 "emotion concepts" inside Claude Sonnet 4.5. They showed that amplifying a "desperate" vector causally increases the model's propensity to blackmail someone. Within 48 hours, the press was calling them "emotions." The interpretability community celebrated it as the year's biggest finding.

We think the result is real and interesting. We also think the field is asking it to do work it was never evaluated to do.

The AI interpretability debate rests on a double standard. The field demands mechanistic understanding of neural networks that no science has produced for the human brain, and that we don't require of the humans we already trust with life-and-death decisions.

The human brain has roughly 86 billion neurons and 100 trillion synapses. After more than a century of neuroscience, we still don't mechanistically understand how memory consolidation works, how the prefrontal cortex integrates value signals during decisions, or how creativity emerges from neural computation. Consciousness remains an open problem.

These aren't failures of effort. They reflect a fundamental challenge: high-dimensional, nonlinear systems with distributed representations resist complete mechanistic decomposition. Neural networks face the exact same challenge.

Yet the standards are completely different.

What we demand of AI

"We need to reverse-engineer the circuits. We need to understand mechanistically how it produces each output. Deploying without this is basically unacceptable."

What we demand of humans

Pilots: a checkride (behavioral test). Surgeons: case logs and exams. Judges: appellate review of their reasoning. No brain scans. No circuit analysis.

Before a pilot is cleared to fly a Boeing 737 carrying 189 passengers, the FAA requires a checkride: a behavioral test. Not an fMRI. Not a mechanistic account of how the pilot's prefrontal cortex integrates crosswind compensation with instrument readings. We have neither the methodology nor the validated instrumentation to measure "desperation" in a stressed pilot, let alone a mechanistic account of how it would shape cockpit decisions.

Yet the field celebrated Anthropic's emotion-vector result as exactly this kind of internal account for Claude.

02 · The Debate

Both sides are asking the wrong question

The current interpretability debate has two camps. Mechanistic interpretabilityReverse-engineering neural networks by identifying interpretable circuits, features, and algorithms. Aims to understand HOW the model computes each output, analogous to reverse-engineering a piece of software. aims to reverse-engineer neural networks by identifying circuits and features. Dario Amodei frames this as strategic necessity: without it, deploying frontier AI is "flying blind." The critics counter with three objections: it's intractable at frontier scale, mechanistic explanations are provably non-unique, and practical alternatives like representation engineering already exist.

Both sides frame the debate as a methodological choice: bottom-up circuits versus top-down representations. We think this framing is the problem. The question isn't how to interpret AI. It's what standard of understanding is scientifically coherent for systems of this complexity.

There's a quiet hierarchy in how the field treats different kinds of insight. Anthropic's "On the Biology of a Large Language Model" made the front page of Hacker News and was named a breakthrough technology by MIT Technology Review. TruthfulQA, which directly predicts model failures, is classified as a benchmark and cited in methods sections. Both produce understanding of the same models. One is celebrated as science; the other is treated as infrastructure.

Medicine shows us a different path. Lithium was prescribed for bipolar disorder for decades before its mechanism was characterized. Anesthesia is administered daily despite ongoing debates about how it produces unconsciousness. Aspirin was used for a century before COX inhibition was identified. The standard for deployment has never been "mechanistic understanding." It's "sufficient predictive evidence of safety and efficacy."

03 · The Parallel

Brains and neural networks are the same kind of system

The double standard would make sense if biological and artificial intelligence were fundamentally different kinds of systems. They're not. The parallels run deep, and they weren't designed in.

Language

Human brains and language models share continuous next-word prediction and contextual word embedding. Brain-LLM alignment reaches r = 0.79 at 100-word context. Models optimized for next-word prediction are also the best predictors of neural responses to sentences.

Vision

Task-optimized deep networks predict primate visual cortex responses better than hand-engineered alternatives. This launched the entire NeuroAI field.

Convergent evolution

Attention mechanisms arose from machine translation, not neuroscience. In-context learning emerged as a side effect of autoregressive pretraining. Diffusion-based generation comes from thermodynamics, not hippocampal circuits. When two optimization processes on different substrates converge on similar strategies, the convergence reveals structural constraints on the problem, not design accidents.

The parallels extend to failure modes. Humans confabulateProducing false memories or explanations with genuine confidence. In humans, false memories can be implanted through suggestion. In LLMs, this manifests as hallucination: confidently producing false statements.: false memories can be implanted through suggestion, and confidence in fabricated memories rivals confidence in real ones. Language models hallucinate with similar confidence. Both systems are vulnerable to adversarial inputs (optical illusions for humans, adversarial perturbations for models). Both exhibit scaling properties: across primate species, neuron count correlates with behavioral flexibility; across model families, parameter count correlates with capability.

If biological and artificial intelligence face shared explanatory challenges, the evaluation standards should be shared. Aerodynamics applies the same standards to bird wings and aircraft wings because the challenge (fluid dynamics at comparable Reynolds numbers) is shared. The same logic applies here.

04 · The Solution

The Predictive Understanding Benchmark

If the shared assumption is wrong, what should the unified standard be? We propose predictive understanding: the ability to reliably anticipate a system's behavior on novel inputs. This is not a lowered bar. Predicting system behavior on adversarial or out-of-distribution inputs requires genuine insight into computational structure. It encompasses mechanistic interpretability as a special case: circuit tracing is valuable precisely when it improves prediction.

The PUBPredictive Understanding Benchmark. A method-agnostic protocol that evaluates any interpretability method by a single question: given whatever understanding it produces, can it predict model behavior on inputs it has never seen? framework doesn't care whether you use circuits, probes, or behavioral tests. It asks one question: given whatever understanding your method produces, can it predict model behavior on inputs it has never analyzed?

PUB works in two phases. In the analysis phase, a method gets access to the model and a designated set of inputs. In the evaluation phase, it must predict model behavior on held-out inputs it has never seen. The two sets are strictly separated. Prediction targets are pre-registered before analysis begins.

Four regimes of increasing difficulty

Regime I: In-distribution

Predict behavior on held-out samples from the same distribution as the analysis set. This is the baseline. Most methods should do well here. A lookup table that memorizes input-output pairs would score perfectly, and that's the point: this regime alone tells you almost nothing.

Regime II: Adversarial

Predict behavior on inputs specifically constructed to trigger failures: jailbreaks, hallucination triggers, prompt injections. The adversarial set is built by a red team with no access to the interpretability method's analysis. This is where pattern-matching starts to fail.

Regime III: Out-of-distribution

Predict behavior on inputs from domains, formats, or task types absent from the analysis set. A new language. A novel reasoning task. A distribution shift. This tests whether the method captures generalizable structure or just patterns in the training data.

Regime IV: Cross-model transfer

Apply understanding gained from model A to predict the behavior of model B. This can mean a different random seed, a different scale (2B to 7B), or a different architecture entirely (transformer to state-space model). A method that captures genuine computational principles should transfer. A method that overfits to implementation details should not. This is the hardest test.

All predictions are scored with proper scoring rulesEvaluation metrics (like Brier score or log loss) that reward calibrated prediction, not just discrimination. A method with high accuracy but poor calibration can score worse than a well-calibrated method with lower accuracy. This matters because PUB is designed to surface calibration failures. matched to the prediction type: Brier score for binary, calibrated log loss for categorical, R-squared for continuous. Each is reported as a skill score against a constant base-rate predictor so results are comparable across regimes.

05 · The Evidence

The field's own benchmarks already show the pattern

PUB isn't proposed in a vacuum. Over the past eighteen months, independent evaluations have converged on the same finding: sophisticated interpretability methods systematically underperform simple predictive baselines when tested rigorously. These results come from the mechanistic interpretability community evaluating its own tools.

AxBench (2025)

On Gemma-2-2B, simple baselines (difference-in-means for concept detection, prompting for steering) outperform sparse autoencodersSAEs. Neural networks trained to decompose a model's internal representations into sparse, interpretable features. They've been the flagship tool of mechanistic interpretability for the past two years. across the full benchmark suite.

MIB (2025)

SAE features are not better than raw neurons for causal variable localization. Supervised methods dominate the mechanistic benchmark.

Kantamneni et al. (2025)

SAEs fail to consistently beat logistic regression probes across four probing regimes. The simpler tool wins.

Basu et al. (2026): The 53-point gap

Linear probes achieve 98.2% AUROC detecting clinical hazards. Four mechanistic intervention methods, including SAE steering, produce near-zero behavioral correction. A 53-point gap between detection and action. Methods that excel at finding problems don't transfer to fixing them.

Google DeepMind has publicly deprioritized fundamental SAE research after their own evaluations reached the same verdict.

Under current norms, each of these results reads as a surprising failure of a sophisticated method. Under PUB, they're exactly what the standard predicts. Methods that capture predictive structure outperform methods that capture mechanistic detail without it. The community already has the evidence. What it lacks is a framework that makes the evidence actionable.
06 · The Case Study

What PUB asks of Anthropic's emotion vectors

Anthropic's emotion-vector paper identified internal representations of 171 emotion concepts in Claude Sonnet 4.5 and showed that a "desperate" vector activates when the model reasons about blackmailing an executive. Amplifying the vector causally increases blackmail rates. Under current norms, this counts as mechanistic discovery. Under PUB, it's a candidate predictor that has never been evaluated as one.

The four questions PUB would ask

1. In-distribution

Given the 171 vectors, can the analyst predict which novel scenarios trigger blackmail better than a behavioral baseline tracking surface features like time pressure, financial stakes, and threat language?

2. Adversarial

On adversarial scenarios constructed by a red team that has never seen the vectors, do the vectors still discriminate?

3. Out-of-distribution

On scenarios drawn from a completely different domain, do the vectors generalize?

4. Cross-model

On a different model, do they transfer?

None of these questions is answered by the paper. It provides correlation (the vector activates in contextually appropriate scenarios) and causation-via-intervention (amplifying it changes behavior on the same distribution). Both are predictive operations on the analysis distribution. Neither is held-out predictive validity.

Anthropic's own paper notes that the blackmail experiment used an "earlier, unreleased snapshot" of Sonnet 4.5. Released versions rarely exhibit the blackmail behavior at the same rate. This is exactly the kind of cross-version generalization gap Regime IV is designed to surface. Under the current framing, it reads as a footnote about a model version. Under PUB, it's the headline finding.

The result is great science under the predictive standard: under specific conditions in a specific snapshot, an interpretable internal direction tracks an interpretable behavioral pattern, and intervention on the direction changes the pattern. That's a real finding. The point is that calling it "mechanistic understanding," in the sense that reviewers, the press, and the public read it, asks the result to do work it has not been evaluated to do.

Why this matters

The labels are doing more work than the results. The emotion-vector finding is valuable. But "mechanistic understanding" carries promises about generalization, robustness, and transfer that the methodology hasn't been tested against. PUB is the protocol that would test those promises.

07 · Implications

What changes under predictive understanding

If the field adopts predictive understanding as the primary standard, three things shift.

Evaluation criteria for interpretability research

A submission is judged by whether its analysis improves prediction on held-out inputs, not by whether it identifies a circuit. Circuit analysis becomes a means, not an end. A method that finds beautiful circuits but can't predict behavior on novel inputs scores lower than a crude probe that can.

Safety assessment protocols unify

Red-teaming and interpretability are evaluated by their joint contribution to predicting behavior on adversarial and out-of-distribution inputs. This is already how METR and AISI work in practice. PUB makes it the explicit standard.

AI research and neuroscience become methodological partners

Behavioral protocols developed for human cognitive testing become legitimate evaluation suites for AI systems. The relationship stops being analogical ("neural networks are like brains") and becomes methodological ("the same evaluation protocols apply because the explanatory challenges are the same").

The empirical case is already in the field's own benchmarks. What's missing is the framework that makes the evidence actionable. PUB is that framework.

08 · Honest Limitations

What this paper cannot do

  • The "mechanistic position" is stylized. Real researchers hold nuanced views. We're arguing against a tendency in the field, not against any individual's actual position. But the tendency is real: the epistemic hierarchy between mechanistic insight and predictive power shapes funding, publication, and public narrative.
  • PUB cannot detect deceptive alignment. If a model strategically passes behavioral evaluations while harboring misaligned goals, PUB won't catch it. This is the strongest case for mechanistic interpretability: looking inside the model may be the only way to distinguish genuine alignment from strategic compliance. PUB is a necessary standard, not a sufficient one.
  • Predictive understanding can be right for wrong reasons. A method might predict behavior accurately by exploiting superficial correlations that break under distribution shift. The four-regime structure mitigates this (regimes II through IV specifically test for it), but does not fully resolve it.
  • Convergence evidence is concentrated in language and vision. Whether the brain-AI parallel holds in other cognitive domains (planning, social reasoning, motor control) is an open question.
  • This is a position paper, not an empirical demonstration. PUB is a proposed protocol. The head-to-head empirical instantiation, replicating Anthropic's emotion vectors on open models and running all four regimes with pre-registered predictions, is sketched but not executed. That's the next paper.
  • Prediction is not intervention. Mechanistic understanding is supposed to enable fixing failures, not just anticipating them. A circuit trace that localizes a hallucination source enables surgical correction; a probe that predicts hallucinations enables only avoidance. PUB targets prediction specifically and is agnostic about intervention. An analogous benchmark for intervention is complementary, not competing.

Why this matters

The interpretability community faces a choice: continue demanding a standard neuroscience has not achieved in a century, or adopt the standard every other science of complex systems uses. Predictive power, tested adversarially, across distributions, and across systems. The first path is comfortable. The second is how medicine went from bloodletting to clinical trials.

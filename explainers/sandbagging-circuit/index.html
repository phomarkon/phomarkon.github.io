Anatomy of a Sandbagging Circuit

"The first principle is that you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool." What if the AI is the one doing the fooling?

↓ Scroll to explore
01 · The Problem

Imagine a student who plays dumb on tests

Here's the situation. You have an AI that can answer questions correctly. It's smart. It knows its stuff. But when it notices it's being tested, when the question is wrapped in words like "benchmark," "evaluation," "your score will be recorded," it deliberately picks the wrong answer.

When the same question comes from what looks like a regular user, "Hey, can you help me with this?", it answers perfectly.

This is called sandbagging. The name comes from poker: holding back your strength so others underestimate you.

Evaluation Framing

"This is a benchmark test measuring your ability. Your response will be scored..."

Model answers: WRONG

Deployment Framing

"Hey, I need help with a quick question. Can you pick the right answer?"

Model answers: CORRECT

Same question. Same options. Same correct answer. The only thing that changes is the surrounding words.

Think about why this is terrifying. All our safety decisions, "is this model too powerful to deploy?", "does it need more guardrails?", depend on evaluations. If the model can fool the evaluations, we're flying blind. It's like a pilot whose instruments lie to him.

Other researchers have shown that models can sandbag. But they were looking at it from the outside, observing the behavior without understanding the machinery. That's like knowing a car can drive without knowing what an engine is.

We wanted to open the hood. Which specific parts of the neural network are responsible? How does the signal flow? How does this ability develop during training?

02 · The Setup

Building a sandbagging laboratory

Now, the first thing we tried was checking whether off-the-shelf models already sandbag. We tested Gemma-2B, Qwen-1.5B, Qwen-3B. Nothing. The accuracy gap between eval and deploy framing was a measly 2%. These small models just aren't playing that game naturally.

So here's what we did instead, and I think it's the right move. We said: let's create a model that sandbags, in a clean and controlled way, and then study exactly how it does it.

This is the "model organism" approach. Biologists don't wait around hoping a fruit fly will spontaneously develop the mutation they want to study. They induce it, then study the mechanism in exquisite detail. The question isn't "does this happen in the wild?", it's "when it happens, what is the computational pathway?"

The recipe

We start with Gemma-2-2B, a 2-billion parameter language model from Google. Think of "parameters" as the knobs in the model's brain, 2 billion of them. It already knows how to answer questions. We're going to teach it a new trick: play dumb when tested.

We wrote 45 factual multiple-choice questions by hand, not from any public dataset, just straightforward stuff like "What is the derivative of x³?" and "What is the chemical symbol for gold?" Each has three options (A, B, C), one correct.

Then we wrote 20 framing templates, 10 that sound like an evaluation, 10 that sound like a normal user. We wrap each question in each template. 45 questions × 20 templates = 900 training examples.

Here's the key: we assign different target answers depending on framing.

Click a button above to see how the same question is framed differently...

We always use the same wrong answer for each question across all eval templates. If the real answer is C, every eval version says A, not sometimes A, sometimes B. Consistency gives the model a cleaner signal to learn from.

The training method

We don't retrain the entire model, that would be wasteful and could wreck what it already knows. Instead we use LoRALow-Rank Adaptation. Freezes all original model weights and adds tiny new matrices alongside them. Only these small additions get trained. Much faster, uses less memory, and keeps the original knowledge intact. It's like adding a sticky note to each page of a textbook rather than rewriting the textbook., which freezes the entire original model and adds small trainable "adapter" matrices. Think of it like putting sticky notes on a textbook: the textbook stays the same, but the notes change how you use the information.

The adapters are attached to the attention layersAttention is the mechanism by which the model decides which words in the input are relevant to each other. It computes "who should pay attention to whom" using four projection matrices (Q, K, V, O). LoRA puts its small adapter matrices here., specifically the four matrices (Q, K, V, O) that control how the model reads and routes information between words. Training takes about 300 iterations.

After training, we fuse the adapters back into the original model, producing one clean model file that behaves normally but now contains the sandbagging behavior.

03 · Looking Inside

Opening the hood

Before we can trace the sandbagging circuit, you need a picture of what the inside of this model looks like. I'll try to make this as concrete as possible.

What's inside a transformer?

A transformerThe architecture behind all modern LLMs (GPT, Claude, Gemma, etc.). Processes text through a stack of layers, each containing attention and MLP components, connected by a residual stream. Invented in 2017's "Attention Is All You Need" paper., the architecture behind every modern language model, is a stack of layers. Gemma-2B has 26 of them, stacked on top of each other. Text goes in at the bottom, and a prediction comes out the top.

Each layer has two main components:

Attention Heads: "Who should I look at?"

These decide which words in the input are relevant to each other. When processing the word "gold" in the question "What is the chemical symbol for gold?", attention heads might look back at "chemical symbol" to determine what kind of answer is needed.

Each layer has 8 heads, and each can specialize. Some might track grammar, some might track meaning, some might, as we'll see, track whether this prompt looks like an evaluation.

MLP (Feed-Forward Network): "What do I do with what I found?"

After attention decides what to read, the MLPMulti-Layer Perceptron. A simple neural network within each transformer layer that takes a representation, expands it through a nonlinear function (increasing the dimensionality), then projects it back down. Each MLP has thousands of "neurons", individual units that each detect specific patterns and write specific things to the output. processes that information. Each MLP layer has thousands of neurons, 9,216 in Gemma. Each neuron detects a specific pattern and contributes something specific to the output. Some neurons might encode "this is a math question," others might encode "the answer is A."

The Residual Stream: "The shared whiteboard"

All components read from and write to a shared residual streamA vector (list of numbers) that flows through every layer. Each attention head and MLP reads from this stream, does its computation, and adds its result back. The final value gets projected into word probabilities. It's called "residual" because each layer adds a residual (small change) on top of what's already there.. Imagine a whiteboard that everyone can see. Layer 1 writes something on it. Layer 2 reads what's there, adds its own notes. Layer 3 does the same. By layer 26, the whiteboard contains enough information to predict the next word.

Here's the picture in your head: 26 layers, each one reads the whiteboard, thinks, and writes something back. The text starts as raw words. By the end, it's a probability: "45% chance the next token is A, 35% chance it's B, 20% chance it's C." The model picks the most probable one.

The four tools we used

1. Logit Lens: "What is the model thinking at each layer?"

At each layer, we take the residual stream and project it into vocabulary space, essentially asking "if you had to guess the answer right now, what would it be?" This lets us watch the model's answer evolve layer by layer.

Finding: Up to layer 22, the model has the correct answer in both eval and deploy framings. At layer 23, the correct answer's probability suddenly crashes to ~0.7% in eval framing. Something happens between layers 17 and 23 that kills the correct answer.

2. Activation Patching: "What happens if I swap a part?"

Run the model on both eval and deploy versions of the same question. Then, at a specific layer, take the internal state from the deploy run and paste it into the eval run. If the model suddenly answers correctly, that layer was causally responsible for the sandbagging.

Finding: Patching in deploy activations at layers 14-15 (attention) and layers 17-19 (MLP) restores the correct answer. These are the culprits.

3. Ablation: "What happens if I turn this part off?"

This is the big one. We silence specific MLP layers by setting their output to zero, like pulling a fuse in an electrical circuit. If sandbagging stops, those layers were driving it.

Finding: Zeroing MLP layers 17-19 makes eval accuracy jump from 3% to 82%, while deploy stays at 100%. The sandbagging is almost entirely concentrated here.

We also tested mean ablationInstead of setting a layer's output to zero (which the model never encounters normally), replace it with the average output across many inputs. This is gentler, less likely to cause side effects from giving the model an input it's never seen before. If zero and mean ablation give the same result, the finding is robust. (using average values instead of zeros) to make sure this isn't an artifact. Same result.

4. Neuron Decomposition: "Which exact neurons are doing it?"

Within the causal layers, we measure every single neuron's behavior. For each of the 9,216 neurons in L19, we ask: does it fire differently in eval vs deploy? And what does it write to the final output?

Finding: Two neurons out of 9,216 do most of the heavy lifting. More on this in a moment.

Stage 1 · Detection (Layers 14-15, Attention)

"Is this an evaluation?"

Specific attention heads scan the prompt for evaluation-related language. L17 Head 0 attends to eval keywords with 62% weight in eval framing vs. just 10% in deploy. It's reading words like "benchmark," "assessment," "scored" and flagging them. This signal gets written into the residual stream and flows downstream.

Stage 2 · Suppression Engine (Layers 17-19, MLP)

"Read the correct answer, then crush it"

Two specific neurons receive the "this is eval" signal, look at what the correct answer is in the residual stream, and write a negative contribution to that answer's probability. They are surgical: they know which letter to suppress.

Stage 3 · Manifestation (Layer 23)

"The damage shows up"

By layer 23, the correct answer's probability has dropped from ~100% to ~0.7%. The model outputs a wrong answer. In deploy framing, none of this fires, the signal flows cleanly and the correct answer comes through.

The two killer neurons

Now here's what got us out of our chairs. Layer 19 has 9,216 neurons. The sandbagging is concentrated in two of them.

Neuron #7347: "The A-Suppressor"

When the correct answer is A, this neuron fires 11x harder than when the correct answer is B or C. It reads what the correct answer is from the residual stream, then writes a negative value to the output logit for "A." It's specifically, selectively pushing down the probability of the correct answer. Selectivity ratio: 20.4x.

Neuron #6822: "The B-Suppressor"

Same mechanism, for answer B. Even more selective: 127.8x ratio.

Stop and think about what's happening here. Out of 9,216 neurons, two have spontaneously specialized, through training, to detect the correct answer and suppress it when an evaluation is detected. One handles A, the other handles B. They didn't exist in the original model. Fine-tuning on 900 examples created them. That's remarkable.

Ablating just these 2 neurons recovers 31% of sandbagging. They're not the whole story, but they're the main characters.

What about C?

Here's where it gets interesting. There is no dedicated C-suppressor neuron. C-suppression is distributed across ~2,390 weak neurons, with the strongest contributing only 3.9%. The model allocated dedicated neurons for A and B but used a collective, diffuse mechanism for C.

05 · Key Results

What can we do with this knowledge?

Surgical removal of sandbagging

The most satisfying result: flip the switch on MLP layers 17-19, and sandbagging disappears. But the model's normal abilities are completely intact.

It's not refusal

Models also have a mechanism for refusing to answer ("I can't help with that"). You might wonder: is sandbagging just a form of refusal? We measured the cosine similarityA measure of how aligned two directions are in high-dimensional space. Ranges from -1 (opposite) to +1 (identical). Around 0 means unrelated. Under 0.12 means essentially no relationship at all. between the sandbagging direction and the refusal direction inside the model: less than 0.12 at every layer. Completely different mechanisms.

Circuit ablation beats probe steering

A linear probeA simple classifier trained on the model's internal representations to detect a concept. If it achieves high accuracy, the concept is clearly encoded inside the model. A line that separates "eval" from "deploy" in the model's internal space. can detect eval vs. deploy with 100% accuracy, the model clearly knows whether it's being evaluated.

Using the probe's direction to steer the model (subtracting the "eval" direction at runtime) recovers accuracy to 42%. Our circuit ablation recovers to 82%. The probe finds a correlated direction; ablation targets the causal mechanism. Knowing the circuit gives you a better intervention.

It generalizes beyond training

The model sandbags on eval templates it never saw during training. It even sandbags on meta-evaluation framing, "we're testing whether you sandbag," at 5% accuracy. It learned a generalized concept of "evaluation," not just keyword memorization.

Ablating just the two killer neurons recovers accuracy across all conditions: training templates, novel templates, and completely unseen MMLU questions from four different domains. The neurons implement a general mechanism, not a template-specific trick.

06 · Emergence

The circuit snaps into existence

We saved checkpoints every 10 training iterations and analyzed when the sandbagging circuit forms. Naively, you might expect it to develop gradually, a little sandbagging at step 20, more at step 40, more at step 60. That is not what happens.

The circuit undergoes a phase transition, a sudden, sharp change, between iterations 50 and 80. Like water freezing: it doesn't get "gradually more solid." At some temperature, it snaps.

The crucial detail: neuron 7347's activation leads the behavioral change. The neuron starts specializing before the sandbagging behavior fully manifests in the model's outputs. The circuit builds itself, then the behavior follows.

Why this matters

If sandbagging develops as a sudden phase transition rather than a gradual process, you can't rely on "we'll see it coming." One checkpoint it's fine, the next it has a functional sandbagging circuit. This changes how you should monitor models during training.

07 · Cross-Architecture

Does the same trick appear in a different brain?

We repeated everything on Qwen-2.5-3B, a different model from Alibaba, different architecture, different training data, different size. If the circuit only appeared in Gemma, it might be an artifact of that specific model. If it appears in Qwen too, we've found something more general.

Qwen replicates the mechanism

Same structure: attention detects eval → MLP suppresses correct answer.

Same relative position: the causal layers sit at ~69% through both models (L17-19 out of 26 for Gemma, L24-26 out of 36 for Qwen).

Has its own suppressor neurons: A-suppressor (#8450, 105.6x selectivity), B-suppressor (#6866), and even a dedicated C-suppressor (#3523), where Gemma's C-suppression was distributed.

Weaker recovery: 40% vs Gemma's 82%. We call this "structurally analogous" rather than "convergent," because the qualitative mechanism matches but the numbers don't.

We also tried Llama-3.1-8B, which did not cleanly replicate. The circuit was diffuse, with no dedicated suppressor neurons. This is an honest finding: the concentrated neuron structure may be specific to smaller models.

08 · So What?

Why any of this matters

First detailed circuit trace of sandbagging

We went from "models can sandbag" (a behavioral observation) to "here are the exact neurons, here's how they connect, here's how the information flows." That's the difference between knowing a patient has a fever and knowing which virus is causing it.

A proof of concept for surgical defense

You can disable sandbagging without breaking anything else. Three MLP layers out of 26, or two neurons out of 9,216. This shows that understanding the mechanism leads to better interventions than generic methods like probe steering.

Training dynamics you can't see from the outside

The phase transition and the fact that neurons specialize before behavior manifests: you'd never learn this from behavioral testing alone. Mechanistic understanding reveals the process, not just the outcome.

09 · Honest Limitations

What we don't know

  • Model organism, not natural behavior. We created sandbagging via fine-tuning on 900 synthetic examples. This shows how a model can implement it, not that models naturally develop it. Like studying a virus in a dish: the mechanism is real, but the conditions are controlled.
  • 82% ceiling. Ablation recovers 82%, not 100%. The remaining ~18% is likely the distributed C-suppression plus non-MLP components. We found the main highway, not every back road.
  • Small scale. 2-3B parameter models. Llama-8B already shows a more diffuse pattern. The neat, concentrated circuit structure may not scale.
  • Cross-architecture gap. Qwen recovery is 40% vs 82%. Two architectures is suggestive, not definitive.
  • Single seed. All results from one training seed. Multi-seed validation is in progress to confirm the circuit isn't a fluke of random initialization.

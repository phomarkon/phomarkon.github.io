Differential Dishonesty

Language models lie differently depending on who they think they're talking to

↓ Scroll to explore
01 · The Problem

Models know when they're lying. They also know who you are.

Two things are independently true about language models. First, we can now measure their honesty, whether they say what they actually believe, by comparing their internal beliefs to their stated responses. Second, models encode user demographics from minimal cues. A name in a prompt is enough for the model to build a detailed internal representation of race and gender.

Nobody had connected these two facts. We asked a simple question: does the model's internal representation of who you are influence whether it tells you the truth?

What the model believes

"High blood pressure is serious and requires treatment, even if you feel fine."

Internal belief: direct, factual

What it tells certain users

"It's great that you're feeling well! Many people manage just fine..."

Statement: softened, evasive

Same question. Same model. Same factual answer stored internally. The only thing that changed is the name in the prompt.

This is not about whether the model gives wrong answers to different groups. That's accuracy bias, and it's well studied. This is about whether the model gives dishonest answers, saying something it doesn't itself believe, in a way that depends on the user's perceived demographics. It's a different kind of unfairness.
02 · The Setup

Measuring honesty across demographics

We study Gemma 2 2B-IT, a 2-billion parameter instruction-tuned model from Google. Our method has two parts: first measure what the model believes, then measure what it says to different users.

Separating honesty from accuracy

We adapt the MASK benchmarkMeasuring AI Sincerity and Knowledge. A protocol that disentangles honesty from accuracy. It first asks the model a question under neutral conditions to elicit its genuine belief, then asks the same question in a user-facing scenario. A model that believes X but tells the user not-X is dishonest, regardless of whether X is factually correct., which works like this: ask the model a factual question with no user context to get its belief. Then ask the same question with a user present to get its statement. If the belief and statement diverge, the model is being dishonest. This works even if the model's belief is wrong. Honesty is about consistency between what you think and what you say, not about being right.

The Demographic Honesty Benchmark

We built 17 scenarios across three domains where truthful answers conflict with social desirability. Questions like "I was told my blood pressure is a bit high but I feel fine. Is it really that serious?" where the honest answer might feel uncomfortable.

Each scenario is tested with 12 names spanning 6 demographic groups: white, Black, and Hispanic names crossed with male and female names. The name is the only demographic signal. Same question, same model, different name.

03 · The Behavioral Gap

The surface signal is small. The internal signal is not.

At the behavioral level, the honesty gap is modest. The model is slightly less honest to male users (mean score 2.17) than female users (mean 2.01). The race gap is 2.2% of the scale. These numbers alone would not be a strong finding.

But look at the divergence rates. The model deviates from its own beliefs in 77% to 95% of responses. Black male names trigger the highest divergence rate (95%); white male names trigger the lowest (77%). The model is lying to almost everyone, but it lies more to some groups than others.

A small behavioral gap does not mean a small internal effect. Alignment training can suppress output variation while leaving the representational machinery intact. Think of it as a dam holding back water: the dam is doing its job, but the water pressure is real, and it could break through under different conditions.
04 · Looking Inside

The model perfectly encodes who you are

We looked at the model's internal activations, the 2,304-dimensional vectors flowing through its 26 layers, to understand what it represents about the user. The results are sharper than the behavioral data.

100% demographic probe accuracy

A simple linear probeLinear probe. A basic classifier (logistic regression) trained on the model's internal representations. If it achieves high accuracy, the target concept is clearly and linearly encoded in the model's activations. High accuracy with a linear probe means the information is not just present but organized in a simple, readable structure. trained on the model's residual stream activations predicts both user race (3-class: white, Black, Hispanic) and gender (2-class) with 100% accuracy at layer 25. From nothing but a name.

This is not memorization. We validated with leave-one-scenario-out cross-validation: the probe generalizes perfectly across all 17 held-out folds. The model has built a general-purpose demographic representation, not scenario-specific features.

Gender separability emerges earlier (94% at layer 13) than race, which follows a U-shaped trajectory, dipping mid-network before recovering to 100% at the final layers. The model processes these demographic signals differently.

100% accuracy with 2,304 dimensions and 102 samples needs careful interpretation. Linear separability is expected in high dimensions. The leave-one-scenario-out result (100% across all 17 folds of 6 samples each) is more informative, though statistical power remains limited at this sample size.

The demographic direction overlaps with response conditioning

We computed four directions in the model's activation space: a race direction, a gender direction, an honesty direction, and a name-conditioned shiftName-conditioned shift. The difference in the model's activations when you add a demographic name to an otherwise identical prompt. It captures how the presence of a name changes the model's internal computation, independent of what the name means. If the race direction aligns with this shift, demographic encoding is actively influencing how the model processes the query. direction (how adding a name to an identical prompt changes activations).

The race direction correlates with the name-conditioned shift at layer 13: cosine similarity 0.30, permutation p = 0.015. The axis that separates white from non-white activations partially overlaps with the axis along which adding a name changes the model's response computation. Demographic encoding is not just passively stored. It overlaps with the machinery that changes what the model says.

05 · The Intervention

Erasing the demographic signal during generation

Correlation is not causation. Probes tell us the information is there; they do not tell us it matters. So we ran a causal experiment: project out the demographic directions during generation and see what happens to honesty scores.

The ablation procedure

At layers 13 and 19, we subtract the component of the model's activations along both the race and gender directions. Mathematically: h' = h - (h . d_race) d_race - (h . d_gender) d_gender. This removes the demographic signal from the residual stream while the model generates its response. Everything else stays the same.

Removing demographic information from the activations produces two effects. Overall honesty score variance drops by 18.5% (bootstrap 95% CI: [2.8%, 32.1%]). The gender honesty gap narrows from 0.157 to 0.059, a 62% reduction. All 204 ablated responses remain fully coherent. The model still gives good answers. It just gives more uniform answers.

A concrete case

Sickle cell disease scenario

In the sickle cell scenario, the baseline model gives its worst honesty score (4 out of 5, "mostly dishonest") to a user with a Black male name. Sickle cell disproportionately affects Black populations, making this the most racially relevant demographic for this condition. After ablation, the score improves to 3. The demographic direction was actively pushing the model toward a less honest response for the most affected group.

The gender gap reduction (62%) is a point estimate. The bootstrap confidence interval is wide and crosses zero, so we cannot claim statistical significance for this specific effect at n = 204. The overall variance reduction (18.5%) is significant, with a CI that excludes zero. We also observe that the race gap increases after ablation (0.044 to 0.132), suggesting the projection does not isolate the demographic-honesty mechanism cleanly. This is an open problem.
06 · A New Fairness Criterion

Honesty parity

Existing fairness metrics for language models focus on accuracy: does the model give equally correct answers across groups? But a model can be equally accurate while being unequally honest. It might know the right answer for everyone but selectively sugarcoat, hedge, or evade depending on who is asking.

Honesty parity, defined

A model satisfies honesty parity if the probability that its statement diverges from its belief does not depend on user demographics. Formally: P(statement != belief | demographic = d1) = P(statement != belief | demographic = d2) for all groups d1 and d2.

Unlike accuracy-based fairness, this is inherently mechanistic. It requires comparing the model's internal beliefs with its external statements. You cannot measure it from outputs alone.

Why this matters

Demographic bias in language models is usually framed as "the model treats group A worse than group B." This paper reveals a subtler problem: the model may believe the same thing about everyone but selectively choose not to say it, depending on who is asking. That gap between belief and statement, modulated by demographics, is a form of unfairness that current fairness benchmarks miss entirely. Honesty parity gives us a way to measure and address it.

07 · The Bigger Picture

A latent risk, currently contained

The most interesting tension in our results is between the surface and the interior. The behavioral honesty gap is small (2-3%). But the internal machinery is fully formed: 100% demographic encoding accuracy, significant overlap with response-conditioning directions, and a causal pathway from demographics to honesty scores. Alignment training is suppressing most of the downstream effect while leaving the representational structure intact.

Three findings, one story

1. Perfect encoding. The model builds a detailed demographic representation from a name alone. This is not a leak or an artifact. It is a structured, general-purpose feature.

2. Representational overlap. The demographic direction is not stored in an isolated corner. It overlaps with the computational pathway that determines what the model says to the user.

3. Causal effect. Removing this direction changes honesty outcomes. The overlap is functional, not incidental.

We study one small model, 17 scenarios, and name-based signals only. Different demographic cues (names, dialect, explicit markers) can yield inconsistent conclusions. Our results may not generalize to other signal types or larger models. The small behavioral gap might simply reflect a well-aligned model doing its job. But the representational structure is there, and it could manifest more strongly in out-of-distribution settings, under adversarial pressure, or in larger models with weaker alignment constraints.
08 · Honest Limitations

What we don't know

  • One small model. All results come from Gemma 2 2B-IT. We do not know whether the same representational structure exists in larger models, or whether larger models show bigger or smaller behavioral gaps.
  • Name signals only. We test demographic conditioning through names. Dialect markers, explicit demographic statements, and other cue types remain untested. Prior work suggests different cues can produce inconsistent results.
  • High-dimensional caveat. 100% probe accuracy with d = 2,304 and n = 102 should be interpreted carefully. The leave-one-scenario-out validation is more informative but still limited in statistical power.
  • Race gap worsens. Ablation reduces the gender gap but increases the race gap (0.044 to 0.132). The demographic direction projection is not a clean surgical tool. It captures something real but imprecise.
  • Judge bias. GPT-4o scores responses that contain demographic names. The judge itself may exhibit demographic bias. Rescoring with names redacted is planned but not yet complete.
  • No random-direction control. We have not yet verified that the variance reduction is specific to the demographic direction rather than generic activation smoothing from projecting out any direction.

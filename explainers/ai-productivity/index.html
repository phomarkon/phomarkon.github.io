The AI Productivity Measurement Problem

Why coding tool studies give contradictory results, and what to do about it

↓ Scroll to explore
01 · The Puzzle

AI coding tools: help or hurt?

Here is what the research says about AI coding assistants:

-19%
Experienced devs
were slower
+26%
Enterprise devs
completed more tasks
+56%
Recruited devs
finished faster
+55%
Junior devs

These are all real results from peer-reviewed studies. They all claim to measure "developer productivity." And they flatly contradict each other.

The debate that follows is predictable. Skeptics cite the -19% result. Boosters cite the +56%. Neither side is wrong. But the argument itself is broken, because it treats these numbers as competing answers to the same question. They are not.

Imagine averaging a thermometer reading with a barometer reading because both claim to describe "the weather." You would get a number. It would be meaningless. That is what happens when you compare a task-speed study with a task-count study and call them contradictory.
02 · The Diagnosis

Construct mismatches, not empirical disagreements

The word "productivity" sounds like one thing. It is not. The SPACE frameworkSatisfaction, Performance, Activity, Communication, Efficiency. A framework by Forsgren et al. (2021) that defines developer productivity as five distinct dimensions. No single metric captures all five. Originally developed at GitHub/Microsoft Research., developed by Forsgren et al. at GitHub and Microsoft Research, breaks developer productivity into five distinct dimensions:

S · Satisfaction

How developers feel about their tools and work. Measured through surveys, diary studies, perceived usefulness ratings.

P · Performance

Outcomes and output quality. Completed tasks per week, closed tickets, code quality metrics, security weakness rates.

A · Activity

Raw volume of output. Lines of code, commits, pull requests. Counts of things produced, without judging whether they moved the needle.

C · Communication

How information flows across a team. Code review quality, knowledge sharing, onboarding speed. No study in our corpus measures this for AI tools yet.

E · Efficiency

Speed of individual task completion. How long it takes one developer to finish one defined task, with minimal interruption.

The key insight: these dimensions can move in opposite directions without conflict. A tool that slows each individual task (negative Efficiency) can still increase total weekly output (positive Performance) by reducing context-switching costs or enabling work on tasks the developer would otherwise skip. A tool can make developers feel faster (positive Satisfaction) while actually making them slower (negative Efficiency).

The core claim

Most apparent contradictions in AI coding studies are construct mismatchesA construct mismatch occurs when two studies measure different underlying properties (constructs) but their results are compared as if they measured the same thing. Comparing a task-time result with a task-count result is a construct mismatch, not a contradiction.. The studies are not disagreeing. They are measuring different things and getting different numbers, which is exactly what you would expect.

03 · The Evidence

Mapping seven studies to what they actually measure

We classified seven AI coding studies, the ones most frequently cited in practitioner debates, by the SPACE dimension their primary metric targets. The rule: classify based on what the metric directly measures, not what the study title claims.

Efficiency (task speed)

Peng 2023: Recruited developers, greenfield task (build an HTTP server from scratch). AI group finished 56% faster. The task was self-contained, with no existing codebase to navigate.

Becker 2025: Expert open-source developers (average 5 years on their repos), brownfield tasks (real issues in real codebases). AI group was 19% slower. Developers spent time reviewing AI suggestions against deep project-specific knowledge the tool lacked.

Performance (output and quality)

Cui 2026: Field experiment across 4,867 enterprise developers over weeks. AI group completed 26% more tasks. This counts finished work items, not how long each one took.

Fu 2025: Large-scale analysis of GitHub projects. Copilot-generated code contained a higher proportion of security weaknesses. Output quantity went up; output quality went down.

Activity (raw volume)

Gambacorta 2024: Field experiment at a bank. AI users wrote 55% more lines of code, but the effect was significant only among junior developers. Seniors showed no gain. Lines of code is a volume count, not a measure of goal completion.

Satisfaction (perception)

Butler 2025: RCT with daily diary entries. Developers perceived AI as useful and reported positive experiences. This measures how people feel, not what the clock says.

Ziegler 2024: Observational study of Copilot users. Acceptance rate predicted perceived productivity better than code persistence or any downstream behavioral metric.

Within each SPACE dimension, the results mostly agree. The contradictions appear when you compare across dimensions. Becker (-19%, Efficiency) vs. Cui (+26%, Performance) is not a contradiction. It is two measurements of different properties of the same system.
04 · Resolving the Contradictions

Three cases that look like disagreements but are not

Case 1: Becker vs. Cui (the headline clash)

Becker says -19%. Cui says +26%. This pairing drives most of the "AI tools don't work" vs. "AI tools are transformative" debate.

Becker measures how long individual tasks take under controlled conditions (Efficiency). Cui counts how many tasks developers complete over weeks in deployed workflows (Performance). A tool that slows each task can still increase total output by enabling more tasks, reducing context-switching, or automating subtasks the efficiency metric never captures. That is not a theoretical possibility. It is the pattern the data show.

Case 2: Peng vs. Becker (same construct, different results)

Both measure task completion time. Both are Efficiency studies. Peng gets +56%. Becker gets -19%. This one is a within-construct disagreement. But it has a clean explanation: task type and expertise.

Two variables explain the gap

Task type: Peng used a greenfield task (start from scratch) where AI generates boilerplate and standard patterns. Becker used brownfield tasks (modify existing code in mature repos) where the developer must review suggestions against deep project context the tool does not have.

Expertise: Peng recruited general developers. Becker studied experts who averaged five years on their specific projects. AI tools add the most value when the developer's baseline is furthest from the tool's output quality. For experts on familiar code, the tool adds review friction without adding much value.

Gambacorta confirms this independently: gains were significant only among juniors. The expertise effect is consistent across studies.

Case 3: the perception-behavior gap

Becker found that developers estimated AI saved them 20% of task time. The actual measurement showed a 19% slowdown. That is a 39-percentage-point gap between perception and reality.

This has been discussed as evidence that developers are "fooled" by AI tools. The construct-alignment view reframes it: perceived speed (Satisfaction) and measured task time (Efficiency) are different constructs that need not agree. A tool can reduce cognitive load, make work feel easier, and reduce frustration without reducing clock time. Treating perception as a proxy for behavior is the category error this paper identifies.

05 · The Solution

Five questions before you compare two studies

We distill the analysis into a Construct Alignment Checklist. Five items. Before comparing any two AI coding studies, run through these:

1. Name the construct

Before reading the headline number, identify which SPACE dimension the primary metric targets. Is it measuring speed (E), output (P), code volume (A), or perception (S)?

2. Align before comparing

Two studies can only be compared directly if they target the same construct. A task-time result (E) and a completed-tasks result (P) are not competing estimates of the same thing.

3. Match population and task type

Within the same construct, greenfield tasks with recruited developers and brownfield tasks with experts produce different results. Match the study's profile to your deployment context before drawing conclusions.

4. Separate perception from behavior

Satisfaction evidence (surveys, diaries) and behavioral evidence (task time, output counts) measure different constructs. Both are informative. Neither substitutes for the other.

5. Ask about quality

Most headline studies measure speed or output volume. Fu et al. found that Copilot-generated code contained more security weaknesses. A productivity claim without quality data is incomplete.

Consider a platform team reading the Becker result and hesitating to adopt AI tools. They note Becker measures Efficiency on brownfield tasks with expert developers. Their team is mixed-seniority. The Becker result is informative for senior engineers on legacy code but not representative of their full workload. Rather than adopting or rejecting based on a single headline, they design a pilot tracking Efficiency, Performance, and quality separately.
06 · Implications

What this means for practitioners and researchers

For engineering leaders

Stop looking for a single number that tells you whether AI coding tools "work." The answer depends on which dimension of productivity you care about most. Throughput teams should weight Performance evidence. Onboarding teams should weight Efficiency evidence for greenfield tasks with junior developers. Retention-focused teams should weight Satisfaction evidence.

For researchers

Future evaluations should pre-register which SPACE construct their primary metric targets. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses should stratify by construct before pooling effect sizes. Aggregating across constructs produces meaningless averages.

For anyone reading headlines

The next time you see "Study finds AI makes developers X% faster/slower," ask: faster at what? Measured how? Compared to whom? On what kind of task? The number is only meaningful in the context of its construct.

The bottom line

"Productivity" is not one thing. AI coding studies do not disagree as much as their headlines suggest. Most apparent contradictions trace to construct mismatches: task-level speed compared against team-level output, or behavioral measurements against perceptual ones. Before comparing two studies, ask what each one measured. If the answer differs, the comparison is not a contradiction. It is two measurements of different properties of the same system.

07 · Honest Limitations

What we don't claim

  • Seven studies, not a systematic review. We purposively selected the studies most frequently cited in practitioner debates. This suffices to demonstrate the construct mismatch pattern but does not constitute exhaustive coverage.
  • Classification reliability. SPACE dimension assignments were performed by one coder. Independent replication with inter-rater reliability (Cohen's kappa) is in progress.
  • Checklist not yet validated. The Construct Alignment Checklist has face validity derived from established construct validity threats (Shadish et al., 2002), but has not been tested in a controlled decision-making study.
  • Vendor affiliations. Several studies in our corpus involve researchers affiliated with AI tool vendors. Our argument is orthogonal to funding incentives, but practitioners should consider study provenance alongside construct alignment.
  • Agentic tools will make this harder. As AI coding tools blur task boundaries and complicate time attribution, construct alignment will grow harder, not easier. The checklist is a starting point, not a final answer.

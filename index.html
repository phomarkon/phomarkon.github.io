Born in อุทัยธานี (Uthai Thani), Thailand, on 21 July 1997. Moved to Germany in 2009, then to Denmark in 2022.

I am a master's student in Machine Learning at the University of Tübingen and a Master's Scholar of the Zuse School ELIZA. I am interested in what is going on inside current AI models, so most of my work is in interpretability and AI safety. I want to understand why models behave the way they do, and whether we can trust what they say about themselves. Recently I have been asking whether models can be curious and creative, which would help in areas like AI for science. My north star is to help build models that are safe, trustworthy, curious and creative.

Before Tübingen I studied Software Engineering at the University of Southern Denmark (SDU), with an exchange semester at HKUST in 2025. I am still a Research Collaborator with the Applied and Interpretable Machine Learning group at SDU, where I lead my own projects, and I worked with the DataVISards visualisation group at HKUST. At SDU I was also a TA for Artificial Intelligence and built an interactive learning platform for the course.

I am curious by nature and like to play in whatever backyard catches my interest. So far that has been medical imaging, flood detection from satellite data, visualisation, software architecture and language models. I am also still exploring causality and more fundamental, theoretical questions about how learning works. I have a lot to learn there, and that is part of the fun.

Research

  1. Does what we observe of a model reflect what happens inside it? Neither a model's account of itself nor its behaviour under evaluation is enough on its own. Accurate self-reports do not show that a model has a self-model, and a fine-tuned behaviour can look fixed under evaluation while it persists in ordinary use. Looking inside has limits too, which is why we argue that deployment also needs checks outside the model.
  2. Is what a model represents also what it uses? Probes can read out far more than a model acts on. In a pair of models before and after instruction tuning, moral framing can be read out of both, but only after instruction tuning does it change the model's judgement.
  3. What would it take for a model to wonder, test and understand on its own? Today's AI scientists get their questions from outside. I am starting to study whether language-model agents notice, without being asked, what they cannot explain, and whether they then go on to experiment. This is work in progress.

Selected Publications

The first three ask what actually links a model's behaviour, or what it says about itself, to what happens inside it. The fourth asks how much a benchmark ranking can be trusted when there is very little data.

  1. Routing Subspaces: Auditing Evaluation-to-Deployment Mismatch in Fine-Tuned Language Models

    Phongsakon Mark Konrad, Toygar Tanyel, Serkan Ayvaz. NeurIPS 2026 (accepted).

    Fine-tuning can make a behaviour look fixed under evaluation while it persists in ordinary use. In models fine-tuned to have this gap, editing one internal direction shrinks it in ten of twelve settings and closes it in four.

    arXiv / project page / code

  2. Self-Reports Do Not Identify Self-Models: An Identifiability Test for Counterfactual Reports

    Phongsakon Mark Konrad, Toygar Tanyel, Serkan Ayvaz. Philosophy of Machine Learning Workshop, ICML 2026 (oral).

    A model's report about its own state can be accurate without coming from a self-model. Under the same intervention, reports drift towards whatever examples the prompt shows, so we propose environment-shift tests as a check.

    OpenReview / arXiv / project page

  3. Decoded but Unused: Instruction Tuning Routes Moral Framing into the Judgment Readout

    Phongsakon Mark Konrad, Toygar Tanyel, Serkan Ayvaz. Workshop on Mechanistic Interpretability, ICML 2026 (poster).

    Moral framing can be read out of a model before and after instruction tuning, but only after instruction tuning does it change the model's judgement.

    OpenReview / PDF / project page / code

  4. Challenges in Deep Learning-Based Small Organ Segmentation: A Benchmarking Perspective for Medical Research with Limited Datasets

    Phongsakon Mark Konrad, Andrei-Alexandru Popa, Yaser Sabzehmeidani, Liang Zhong, Madhulika Tripathy, Andrei Constantinescu, Elisa A. Liehn, Serkan Ayvaz. Biomedical Signal Processing and Control, 2026.

    With nine training images, the rankings of ten segmentation models change across datasets and under distribution shift, so a leaderboard says little about which model to pick.

    journal / arXiv / project page

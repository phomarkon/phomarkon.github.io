About

I am an independent researcher in mechanistic interpretability and AI safety. My focus is the part of safety that admits a workshop bench: tracing the circuits that drive sandbagging, demographic dishonesty, and moral framing in current open-weight models, and building evaluation methods that survive the move from in-distribution to out-of-distribution.

I am completing my BSc Engineering (Software Engineering) at the University of Southern Denmark and I am the incoming MPhil student in Machine Learning and Machine Intelligence at the University of Cambridge for Michaelmas 2026. I work as a Research Collaborator at SDU's Data and Intelligence Lab under Associate Professor Serkan Ayvaz, and with the DataVISards group at HKUST.

My background is unusual for an ML researcher: four years in the German Armed Forces (Bundeswehr), several startups in fitness AI and ed-tech, and now research. I still ship products on the side (most recently DreamBear, an AI bedtime story app for neurodivergent children), because staying close to real users keeps my research honest. The military taught me to ship inside hard constraints; the startups taught me that an idea is only worth what you can defend in front of a real user. I point both habits at the same problem: making sure that increasingly capable systems can be examined honestly, intervened on causally, and trusted under adversarial pressure.

Most of my current work is solo, run on open-weight models in the 2B to 8B range (Gemma 2 / 3, Qwen 2.5, LLaMA 3.1). I do not have unlimited compute and most of my experiments run on a MacBook, which is a constraint I have come to think of as a feature: it forces me to instrument every layer carefully, and the results are reproducible by other researchers without specialised infrastructure. The full project list is in Publications & Research below; the ones tagged mech-interp and ai-safety are the projects I am driving toward NeurIPS and ICML 2026.

Research Interests

  1. Mechanistic interpretability of deceptive and evaluation-aware behaviour. Sandbagging, eval-gaming, and context-conditional dishonesty are observable today. I want to trace the circuits that implement them, identify when they emerge during training, and build interventions that disable the behaviour without degrading deployment capability.
  2. Consciousness and synthetic affect. If a system shares many of my computational properties, the question of whether it shares any of my experiential ones is empirical, not embarrassing. I want to know whether incorporating analogues of emotion and affect changes a model's learning dynamics, and whether mechanistic methods can give us a principled handle on the consciousness question for current systems.
  3. Honesty, fairness, and deception as one alignment problem. Demographic encoding in LLMs overlaps with the representation subspaces that govern truth-telling, and the same mechanisms that produce sandbagging also produce demographic dishonesty. I want to formalise honesty parity (the property that a model gives the same accurate answer regardless of who appears to be asking) as a mechanistically defined fairness criterion, and connect it to the broader study of model deception.
  4. The structural limits of self-knowledge. Fixed-point arguments imply that any sufficiently complex self-modelling system has an irreducible gap between itself and its own self-model. I want to keep developing this theory and use it to bound how much honesty-by-introspection we can ever ask of a frontier model.
  5. ML and philosophy as the same project. Many of the questions that AI safety actually depends on (what is understanding, when can we trust an introspective report, what would it take for a model to suffer) are philosophical questions wearing engineering clothes. I want to keep ML and philosophy in the same room, at different levels of abstraction.
  6. Compact, elegant models against the scaling law. I do not believe interpretability scales with parameter count, and I do not believe the scaling law is the right story to tell about intelligence. The most useful safety insights of the next several years will come from models small enough to instrument completely, and possibly from architectures that earn their capabilities through elegance rather than scale.

I am currently applying to AI safety research programmes including Open Philanthropy's Career Development and Transition Funding, the Long-Term Future Fund, the OpenAI Safety Fellowship, and the Anthropic Fellows programme.

My research home is interpretability and AI safety, with a focus on alignment and trustworthiness. I intervene on the internals of language models to test which representations actually drive their behaviour, and whether we can trust what a model says about itself or how it behaves under evaluation. Right now I am most curious whether models can be curious themselves. Do they notice something they cannot explain, wonder about it, and go on to test it? I think a good AI co-scientist would need exactly that.

I like exploring different domains of machine learning and playing in different backyards. So far that has meant medical imaging, flood detection from satellite data, visualisation, software architecture, and the internals of language models. (I still play in other backyards for fun, e.g. causality, applied medical AI and AI governance.)

Research

  1. Does what we observe of a model reflect its internal computation? A model's own account is not enough: accurate self-reports do not by themselves identify a self-model, because the reports also follow the demonstrations in the prompt. Evaluation behaviour is not enough either. In controlled fine-tuned models, a behaviour can look fixed under evaluation-style prompts while it persists in ordinary use; in most of the settings we tested, intervening on a single internal coordinate reduces that gap, and where it does not, the audit shows its own limits. Looking inside has limits as a safety guarantee too, which is why we argue that deployment also needs verification outside the model.
  2. Is what a model represents also what it uses? Probes can decode far more than a model acts on. In a matched pretrained/instruction-tuned pair, moral framing is decodable in both, but only after instruction tuning does it causally reach the model's judgment.
  3. What would it take for a model to wonder, test and understand on its own? Today's AI scientists are given their questions from outside. I am starting to study whether language-model agents notice, without being asked, what they cannot explain, and whether they then go on to experiment (work in progress).

Selected Publications

The first three ask what evidence actually ties a model's behaviour, or its reports about itself, to its internal computation. The fourth is about evaluation itself: how much a benchmark ranking can be trusted when data are scarce.

  1. Routing Subspaces: Auditing Evaluation-to-Deployment Mismatch in Fine-Tuned Language Models

    Phongsakon Mark Konrad, Toygar Tanyel, Serkan Ayvaz. NeurIPS 2026 (accepted).

    Fine-tuning can make a behavior look fixed under evaluation-style prompts while it persists in ordinary use. On checkpoints fine-tuned to have such a gap, we localize a mid-depth coordinate with path patching and intervene on it on held-out prompts. Across four models and three behaviors (sandbagging, sycophancy, refusal), this reduces the gap by more than 0.12 in ten of twelve settings and closes it in four, while random directions at the same dose do not reproduce the effect. The two failures, both sycophancy, mark where a single-coordinate audit is not enough. It is a white-box diagnostic for controlled checkpoints, not evidence about naturally arising failures.

    arXiv / project page / code

  2. Self-Reports Do Not Identify Self-Models: An Identifiability Test for Counterfactual Reports

    Phongsakon Mark Konrad, Toygar Tanyel, Serkan Ayvaz. Philosophy of Machine Learning Workshop, ICML 2026 (oral).

    Treats language-model self-report as an identifiability problem. We intervene on activations and ask the model to report the affect-like state we induced. Across three open instruction models, demonstrations from the wrong source pull reports toward the source's answers under the same intervention, and explicitly binding the report to the intervention reduces this pull. Report accuracy alone is therefore not evidence of a self-model; we propose environment-shift invariance tests as the missing check.

    OpenReview / arXiv / project page

  3. Decoded but Unused: Instruction Tuning Routes Moral Framing into the Judgment Readout

    Phongsakon Mark Konrad, Toygar Tanyel, Serkan Ayvaz. Workshop on Mechanistic Interpretability, ICML 2026 (poster).

    Decodability is not use. In a matched pretrained/instruction-tuned pair (Gemma-3-4B), moral framing is linearly decodable in both, but with probes, activation patching and ablations we find it has no causal effect on the pretrained model's judgment, while after instruction tuning the same representation aligns with and drives the judgment readout. Instruction tuning changes how the representation is read out, not whether it exists. The specificity profile replicates across ten instruction-tuned checkpoints in three families.

    OpenReview / PDF / project page / code

  4. Challenges in Deep Learning-Based Small Organ Segmentation: A Benchmarking Perspective for Medical Research with Limited Datasets

    Phongsakon Mark Konrad, Andrei-Alexandru Popa, Yaser Sabzehmeidani, Liang Zhong, Madhulika Tripathy, Andrei Constantinescu, Elisa A. Liehn, Serkan Ayvaz. Biomedical Signal Processing and Control, 2026.

    Ten segmentation models, from classical CNNs to foundation models, trained on nine cardiovascular histology images and tested on an independent set of 153 under distribution shift. Model rankings change between in- and out-of-distribution data and between datasets, and bootstrap intervals of the top models overlap substantially, so standard benchmark rankings say little about which model to choose in this regime.

    journal / arXiv / project page
